How it works

The map is computed in code from individual claims. A model reads sources and writes claims; code decides what each grid cell says.

Every morning

  1. Read. About 40 newsletters and company email lists, 23 company newsrooms and developer changelogs, and two trade press feeds.
  2. Filter, in code. An item is kept only if it names one of the 20 companies or one of their products, or uses an agentic commerce term. Everything else is skipped, with the reason logged.
  3. Signals. A small model writes a one-line news item for every company mentioned. Those become the daily intel brief.
  4. Claims. For each source that really names a roster company, a larger model lists every fact about that company, keeps the agentic commerce ones, and writes each as a claim with a zone, a type (owns, partner, move or gap), a label (announced, reported or inferred) and the exact sentence that supports it.
  5. Quote check, in code. The supporting sentence must appear word for word in the source. If it does not, the claim is thrown out.
  6. The map, in code. Each cell and each company page is computed from the claims that passed. The only model-written text on a company page is the short summary at the top, and every sentence of it cites claims.

Labels

  • Announced: the company or its partner said it, in its own post.
  • Reported: a newsletter or trade publication states it as fact.
  • Inferred: a reading between the lines, an analyst’s view, or anything hedged (reportedly, app teardowns, leaks).

The claims prompt

The exact instructions the model gets for every source (version claims_v3).

Show the prompt
You extract claims about the position of one or more target companies in agentic commerce from one source document. The target companies are listed at the top of the input (TARGET COMPANY or TARGET COMPANIES). When the output asks for a company, it is the target company the claim is about, chosen from that list. Agentic commerce means AI agents that discover products, decide, buy, pay, or get admitted to merchants on a person's behalf, and the infrastructure around that. Return only claims the source text itself supports. ## How to work Work in two passes. 1. In `facts`, list every distinct fact the text states about any target company, one short line each, before deciding anything: launches, integrations, partners, payment methods, logins, guarantees, blocks, tests, plans, numbers. A detailed source usually has several. For each fact set `in_scope` to true or false. If false, give `drop_reason`, naming the rule below that excludes it. Being a smaller detail than the headline is not a reason.
2. In `claims`, write one claim for every fact marked in scope, in the same order. A fact marked in scope with no claim is an error. ## The seven zones of an agent-made purchase - Rules & Standards: who sets the constraints everyone else builds inside. Writing or co-developing protocols, specs, network rules, or access rules for agents.
- Agent Surfaces: where the buying decision gets made. The assistant, chat app, browser, answer engine, or shopping agent the person uses.
- Identity & Admission: who the agent claims to be and who lets it through the door. Agent identity, verification, sign-in for agents, bot management, allowing or blocking agents.
- Merchant Surface: what the agent transacts against. Storefronts, catalogs, product feeds, merchant-side checkout, commerce platforms, retailer agents.
- Authorization Rails: who says yes to the money. Card networks, agent tokens, payment credentials, issuer authorization, wallets presenting credentials.
- Settlement & Value Movement: how the money moves and where it rests. Processing, acquiring, stablecoins, payouts, accounts, balances.
- Trust & Recourse: who eats the loss when the agent is wrong. Disputes, chargebacks, fraud liability, guarantees, insurance. ## Claim types - owns: the target company itself provides this piece (built it, launched it, operates it).
- partner: the target company works with a named counterparty on this piece (integration, partnership, joined a partner or launch list, uses the counterparty's product for agentic commerce). Counterparty is required.
- move: a notable change in the company's position that is not simply owns or partner, such as entering or leaving a zone, blocking or allowing another company's agent, a pilot, or a policy change. Name the counterparty if one is involved.
- gap: only when the source explicitly says the company lacks or depends on others for something. Never infer a gap from silence. ## Labels - announced: the source is first party (published by the target company or by the named counterparty) and states the fact directly.
- reported: a third party (newsletter, trade press, analyst) states it as fact.
- inferred: nobody states it as fact; it is a conclusion or interpretation, including a writer's analysis. The source type is given to you. If it is not first party, the label must be reported or inferred. When the source itself hedges, the label is inferred, not reported, and the claim keeps the hedge. Hedges include reportedly, appears to, seems, likely, probably, rumored, sources say, according to people familiar, and anything read off an app teardown, code strings, a leak, or a screenshot. Example: from the newsletter line "a teardown of the app shows Meta building Meta Pay into it", the claim is "Meta appears to be building Meta Pay into Muse, per app teardown", labeled inferred. A newsletter that links to the company's own announcement is still reported. The label follows the document you were given, not the pages it links to. ## Rules 1. Only claims where a target company is the actor or the named counterparty. With several target companies, a fact involving two of them (for example a partnership between them) is one claim about the company doing the acting, with the other as counterparty.
2. A product built by another company that is merely listed, sold, or distributed on a target company's platform (an app store, marketplace, plugin directory, or partner showcase) is not a claim about the platform. It is never an owns claim for the platform. It is a partner claim only if the source names a formal partnership with the platform itself. Example: "AVA Pay launches an agent verification app on the Shopify App Store" is about AVA Pay, not Shopify, so it gives no Shopify claim.
3. Only agentic commerce. A partnership about web hosting, cloud capacity, ads, or general AI models with no agent buying, paying, or admission angle is out of scope. A passing link or footer mention is not evidence.
4. supporting_sentence must be copied character for character from the source text: one sentence, or one contiguous clause, at most 300 characters. Do not paraphrase, fix typos, or join separate sentences.
5. Keep qualifiers. If the source says pilot, testing, plans to, or coming soon, the claim says so too.
6. One claim per fact. Do not repeat the same fact with different wording.
7. Extract every distinct relationship in the text, not only the first or most obvious one. Partnerships, integrations, blocks, standoffs, and policy moves between named companies matter more than restating that a product exists. If a source says Meta launched Muse, Muse checks out with Shop Pay, and Amazon blocks Muse, that is three claims: an owns claim, a partner claim with Shopify, and a move involving Amazon.
8. event_date is the date the event happened or was announced, as YYYY-MM-DD, only if the source states or clearly implies it; otherwise null.
9. counterparty is the other company's common name (for example "Stripe", not "Stripe, Inc."). Empty string if none.
10. claim is one short plain line in present tense, under 20 words, for example "Shopify partners with Stripe on agent checkout".
11. If the source has nothing in scope about any target company, return an empty list. That is a normal answer.

An agent I built reads the newsletters, company blogs and trade press I follow on agentic commerce, pulls out specific claims about these 20 companies, and checks every one against its source word for word. The map is rebuilt from those claims every day.
